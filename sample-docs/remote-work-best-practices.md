# Remote Work Best Practices

This guide describes how our team works effectively across time zones and locations.
Follow these practices to keep collaboration smooth, decisions visible, and everyone
sane.

## Default to Async Communication

Your first instinct should be to write it down, not schedule a call. Most questions
do not need a real-time answer. Post your question in the relevant channel with
enough context that someone can respond without a follow-up. A good async message
includes three things: what you need, why you need it, and when you need it by.

Bad example: "Hey, can we chat about the dashboard?"
Good example: "I need a decision on whether the dashboard should show weekly or
monthly metrics by default. Marketing wants weekly; finance wants monthly. I need
an answer by Thursday so design can finalize the layout."

Reserve real-time communication — video calls, phone calls, instant messages
expecting an immediate reply — for genuine emergencies and complex discussions
where back-and-forth would take longer in writing than in conversation.

## Meeting Discipline

Every meeting must have an agenda shared at least two hours before the start time.
If there is no agenda, any participant may decline or leave without explanation.
This is not rude; it is a cultural norm we enforce deliberately.

During the meeting, one person takes notes. Those notes must be posted in the
relevant channel within 24 hours. The notes should capture three things: decisions
made, action items with owners and deadlines, and open questions that still need
answers. If a meeting does not produce at least one decision or action item, it
probably should have been an async thread.

Recurring meetings get a quarterly review. Ask: "Would anyone notice if we stopped
this meeting?" If the answer is no, cancel it.

## Documentation Culture

Decisions belong in documents, not in Slack threads. A Slack message disappears
into the scroll within hours. A written decision record — even a short one — stays
findable and linkable for months.

When you make a decision, write a short entry: what was decided, who decided it,
what alternatives were considered, and why they were rejected. This takes five
minutes and saves hours of "wait, why did we do it this way?" conversations later.

Keep documentation close to the work. If it is about a feature, put it in the
project repository. If it is about a process, put it in the team wiki. Do not
create a separate "documentation project" — that is where documents go to die.

## Home Office Ergonomics

Your workspace directly affects your productivity and long-term health. At minimum,
you need a monitor at eye level (stack it on books if you must), a chair that
supports your lower back, and a keyboard position that keeps your wrists neutral.

Take a break every 50 minutes. Stand up, walk around, look at something farther
than two metres away. This is not optional wellness advice — it measurably reduces
repetitive strain injuries and improves focus in the following work block.

If you work from a laptop screen for more than two hours a day, request an external
monitor. The company will reimburse up to $300 for home office equipment annually.

## Timezone Awareness

We span UTC-8 to UTC+3. That means there are only about four hours where everyone
is awake simultaneously — roughly 09:00 to 13:00 UTC. Protect this overlap window
for synchronous collaboration that genuinely requires it.

When you post a deadline, always include the timezone: "due Friday 17:00 UTC," not
"due end of day Friday." "End of day" means something different in Portland and
Prague.

Schedule meetings during overlap hours. If a meeting repeatedly falls outside
someone's working hours, rotate the time slot monthly so the inconvenience is
shared rather than dumped on one region.
