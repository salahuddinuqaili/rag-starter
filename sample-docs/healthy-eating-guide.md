# Healthy Eating Guide

This guide covers the fundamentals of nutrition in plain language. You do not need
a degree in dietetics to eat well — you need a basic understanding of what food
does in your body and a few practical habits.

## Macronutrients

Your body runs on three macronutrients (the big categories of nutrients you need
in large amounts): protein, carbohydrates, and fats.

**Protein** builds and repairs tissue — muscles, skin, hair, enzymes, and immune
cells. Good sources include chicken, fish, eggs, beans, lentils, and Greek yoghurt.
Most adults need roughly 0.8 to 1 gram of protein per kilogram of body weight
daily, more if you exercise regularly.

**Carbohydrates** are your primary energy source. Your brain alone consumes about
120 grams of glucose per day. Choose complex carbohydrates — whole grains, oats,
sweet potatoes, fruits, vegetables — over refined ones like white bread and sugary
drinks. Complex carbs release energy slowly, keeping you full longer and avoiding
the spike-and-crash cycle.

**Fats** are essential for hormone production, brain function, and absorbing
certain vitamins (A, D, E, K). Prioritise unsaturated fats from olive oil, nuts,
avocados, and fatty fish. Limit saturated fats from processed meats and fried
foods. Do not eliminate fat from your diet — your body genuinely needs it.

## The Plate Method

If counting macros sounds tedious, use the plate method instead. Fill half your
plate with vegetables and fruits, a quarter with lean protein, and a quarter with
whole grains or starchy vegetables. This rough ratio gives you a balanced meal
without measuring anything.

## Hydration

Water is involved in nearly every bodily function — digestion, temperature
regulation, joint lubrication, nutrient transport. A reasonable target for most
adults is about 2 litres (roughly 8 cups) per day, adjusted upward if you exercise
or live in a hot climate.

Drink water throughout the day rather than chugging large amounts at once. If you
find plain water boring, add a slice of lemon or cucumber. Coffee and tea count
toward your fluid intake, despite the old myth that caffeine dehydrates you — it
has a mild diuretic effect, but the water in the cup more than compensates.

## Common Myths Debunked

**"Fat makes you fat."** Excess calories make you gain weight, regardless of whether
they come from fat, carbs, or protein. Fat is calorie-dense (9 calories per gram
versus 4 for carbs and protein), so portions matter, but eating fat does not
automatically become body fat.

**"Carbs are evil."** Carbohydrates are your body's preferred fuel source. The
problem is not carbs themselves but the type and quantity — a bowl of oatmeal and a
can of soda are both "carbs," but they affect your body very differently.

**"You need to detox."** Your liver and kidneys are detox organs that work around
the clock. No juice cleanse does what they already do. Eat enough fibre, drink
enough water, and let your organs do their job.

**"Eating after 8pm causes weight gain."** Your body does not know what time it is.
Total calorie intake over the day matters; the clock does not. If eating late leads
to poor choices (chips on the couch at midnight), that is a habit issue, not a
metabolism issue.
